# The Score: How to Stop Playing Somebody Else's Game
[Goodreads Review] (https://www.goodreads.com/review/show/8282055608)

This showed up as one of those "skip a line" books and I greedily snapped it up as I had seen a review of it on the NY Times. I thought it'd be one of those throwaway books and it ended up being one of my favorite "life philosophy" books that I've read in my life.

Disclaimer. I've always been a big video gamer since the first time I could play video games at the arcade in Genting Highlands (a malaysia resort). One of my core memories invovle that relatively smoky room with lots of beeps and boops, my first games of space invaders, or boot hill, or the motorcycle game with the fake motorcycle....so video games (all games really, but especially video games) have that appeal to me that other things (except maybe books) have. So a book that uses video games and map stuff to it is going to inherently appeal more to me.

The author is a philosophy professor and an avid climber and gamer. He talks about why games and score keeping and real life metric keepings (like KPI) feels so different even if they are more or less the same thing. It takes a while to get to his answer, but it comes down to, games are something we voluntarily engage in for fun, so the score is fun to us, but KPI whether in work or life is something that's involuntary (someone imposed that system on you and you don't really have too much input into it), and that's why its not as fun.

It makes sense in a way, mostly because at work, its rare that you get to define a KPI all by yourself. Usually the group has a KPI that has to be aligned with the rest of the company's goals and what you do has to be graded by how well your work impacts those KPIs. There're many other work methods of course, but that's a fairly common one. Another one is soceity's KPIs on children, adults, and senior citizens. When are you supposed to be married, have kids, have a house, and all that stuff. Its why "failure to launch' is even a concept for most societies (NEET is most used as a slur, after all).

The book goes through quite a bit of anecdotes from the author's own life and passions to neatly tie the central thesis together. Where the book fails a bit is the subtitle. It doesn't really tell you how to stop playing somebody else's game. I think ultimately that to stop playing somebody eles's game, you're going to need financial, emotional, and personal security and that's probably beyond the purview of any one book.

Still, I highly recommend this book to anyone who's been entranced by video games, and want to see how maybe they can gamify some parts of their own lives to better suit their world view.
