# The Vegetarian
[Goodreads Review](https://www.goodreads.com/review/show/8611887060)

After reading the other book by Han Kang, I decided to read the novel that won her the Nobel Prize in 2016.

I have to say...I'm not sure what the selection criteria is for the Nobel Prize in literature is, but given that the arts is always a funky subject to judge in, I shouldn't judge too harshly.

Once again, this whole book can be said to be "of the surreal". or "of the escapist". It writes of a woman, who one day, decides to become vegetarian because the taste of meat becomes too sickening to her. Her husband, who does not understand the decision, later decides to divorce her, but not until a lot of trauma is inflicted on the poor woman from her own family (her dad tries to force her to eat meat, which is probably some statement of what the patriarchy is doing to women as a whole...).

The book apparently was published in 3 parts, which makes sense, as each part is told from the Point of view of each character. The first is that of the vegetarian herself, and her struggles with her husband, her own immediate family, and society's reaction to her decisions to stop eating meat.

The second point of view is that from her brother-in-law, who married her sister. He is an artist, and starts to fantasize about the main character from part 1. He uses his art to entice her into having sex with him in such a weird and bizarre manner that you can't really call it cheating, but you also can't not call it cheating. the cheating isn't the point, and more to say that the main character from part 1 really really needed mental health treatment.

The last point of view is that of the sister. Now abandoned by her own cheating husband, and also taking care of her now-in-a-mental institution sister, she's one of the many people who have been caught in the middle. She's also raising a child of her own, trying to hold down a job and taking care of too many people. This is the perspective that I can most relate to, and the least surreal part of the book. The other two parts were too surreal for me, and I frequently just kept thinking the authoress wanted those portions for allegories of something else.

At the end of the day, this book might be a bit too funky for me. I also feel this way about much of Haruki Marukami's books, so maybe its more a failure on my imagination to see the greatness in these books. There is some real message to be had here, but I'm afraid its a bit too much over my head. I do NOT regret having read the book however, and feel that I'm the better for it. Its still a hard one to recommend.

Maybe read this when you're in the mood for less concrete and more surreal trappings.
